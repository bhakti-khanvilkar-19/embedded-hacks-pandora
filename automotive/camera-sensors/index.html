Camera Sensors

Of the three main ADAS sensors, the camera is the only one that "reads" a scene the way a human driver does — recognizing a lane marking as a lane marking, a stop sign as a stop sign, a pedestrian as a pedestrian. Radar and LiDAR confidently say something is there; the camera is what usually says what.

Quick reference
What it is
An image sensor and lens feeding a computer-vision pipeline that detects and classifies lane markings, signs, pedestrians, and vehicles.
Mental model
The one ADAS sensor built to recognize what something is, not just that something is there.
Key numbers
Typically 1–8 MP at 30–60 fps; dynamic range (handling sun glare, headlights, tunnel entrances) matters more day-to-day than raw resolution.
Reach for it when
Classification matters — telling a pedestrian from a shopping cart, or reading a lane marking or a speed-limit sign — not just detecting that an object exists.

From photons to pixels

An automotive camera's image sensor is a grid of photodiodes, each one counting how many photons hit it during the exposure — the same basic photodiode principle behind semiconductor photodetection in general. A color filter over each photodiode (almost always a Bayer pattern of red/green/blue filters) lets the sensor infer color, and an ISP (Image Signal Processor) reconstructs a full-color image from that raw, per-photodiode data before any computer-vision algorithm ever sees it.

Mono vs. stereo: getting depth from a 2D image

A single camera captures a flat, 2D projection — depth isn't directly measured, only inferred (from known object sizes, the ground plane, or a trained model's learned priors). A stereo pair — two cameras a known distance apart — recovers real depth by triangulation, the same baseline-and-angle idea behind radar's angle of arrival, just applied to light instead of radio:

That inverse relationship is also stereo's core limitation: disparity shrinks fast with distance, so stereo depth accuracy is naturally best up close and degrades the farther out an object is — often the exact opposite of where radar's range accuracy shines.

What cameras actually detect

Lane markings
The basis for Lane Departure Warning and Lane Keep Assist — detecting painted lane lines and the vehicle's position relative to them.
Traffic signs and signals
Traffic Sign Recognition (TSR) reads speed limits, stop signs, and similar signage, often displayed back to the driver.
Pedestrians and cyclists
Classification that radar and LiDAR alone struggle to make confidently — critical input to Automatic Emergency Braking.
Other vehicles
Detecting, classifying, and often estimating the pose/orientation of surrounding vehicles for adaptive cruise control and collision-avoidance logic.

Camera vs. radar vs. LiDAR

See the full comparison table on the Radar page rather than repeating it here — the short version is that camera is the classification specialist, at the cost of being the sensor most degraded by darkness, glare, fog, and rain.

Real-world applications

  • Lane Departure Warning / Lane Keep Assist — a forward camera watching lane markings is the primary sensor behind both features.
  • Traffic Sign Recognition — reading speed-limit and regulatory signs and surfacing them on the dashboard.
  • Automatic Emergency Braking — camera-based pedestrian/cyclist classification is frequently what triggers or confirms an AEB event alongside radar's range data.
  • Driver monitoring systems — an inward-facing camera watching the driver's eyes/head pose for drowsiness or distraction, increasingly required by regulation.
  • Surround-view / parking cameras — multiple wide-angle cameras stitched into a top-down view for low-speed maneuvering.

Gotchas

Field notes
  • Camera performance drops sharply in low light, direct sun glare, fog, heavy rain, or simply a dirty or iced-over lens — the sensor most exposed to real-world weather and cleanliness problems of the three.
  • The CNN inference stage is genuinely compute-heavy compared to radar or LiDAR's signal processing — automotive-grade camera systems typically need a dedicated AI accelerator, not a general-purpose CPU alone.
  • Monocular depth estimation is inherently an inference, not a direct measurement — it can be fooled by unusual object sizes or scenes the training data didn't cover, in a way stereo triangulation or radar/LiDAR range measurement can't be.
  • A stereo camera's calibration (the precise baseline and alignment between the two lenses) can drift from vibration or a minor impact over a vehicle's lifetime, silently degrading depth accuracy without an obvious failure symptom — periodic recalibration is a real maintenance concern.
