PSU — Power Supply Unit

Every component on this page's neighboring pages — CPU, GPU, motherboard — needs steady DC power to run at all. The wall only ever provides AC. A PSU is the device that turns one into the other, built directly on the voltage/current fundamentals in Electronics Fundamentals.

Quick reference
What it is
Converts AC mains power into the stable DC voltage rails (+12V, +5V, +3.3V) a computer's components actually need.
Mental model
A translator converting one "language" of electricity (AC from the wall) into several different "dialects" (DC voltage rails) different components speak.
Key numbers
Rated in watts (maximum DC output capacity); efficiency graded 80 Plus Bronze/Gold/Platinum/Titanium.
Reach for it when
Sizing power delivery for a system's total component draw under real, sustained load — not just at idle.

AC to DC

Mains power is alternating current — voltage that reverses direction many times a second. Almost every electronic component needs steady direct current instead — see Electronics Fundamentals for what voltage and current actually are. A PSU's whole job is that conversion: rectifying AC into DC, then regulating it down to the specific steady voltages components expect.

Voltage rails

Wattage and efficiency

A PSU's rated wattage is its maximum DC output capacity, not how much power it draws from the wall — it always draws somewhat more than it outputs, because no conversion is perfectly lossless. The 80 Plus certification tiers (Bronze through Titanium) measure exactly how much extra is wasted as heat during that conversion — a real, ongoing factor in both electricity cost and how much heat a system's cooling has to remove.

Real-world applications

  • Every desktop/server PSU — the case this page describes directly.
  • Laptop and phone chargers — the same AC-to-DC job, done externally, at much lower wattage.
  • Embedded device power supplies — a small embedded system often uses a simple AC adapter or an onboard regulator doing the same AC-to-DC or DC-to-DC conversion at a tiny fraction of the scale — see Embedded.

Gotchas

Field notes
  • Undersizing a PSU for a system's actual peak draw causes instability specifically under load, not at idle — a system that seems fine browsing but crashes under a heavy GPU workload is a classic symptom.
  • A high efficiency rating is not the same as sufficient wattage capacity — a very efficient PSU can still be badly undersized for a power-hungry system.
  • PSU failure is a genuine fire/safety risk category, not just a reliability one — it's one of the few components worth never buying on price alone.
