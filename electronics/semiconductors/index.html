Electronics

Semiconductors — diodes, transistors, and MOSFETs

Everything in Passive Components only resists, stores, or releases energy. Semiconductors are where electronics gets interesting — a material that can be switched between conducting and not conducting is what makes diodes, transistors, and ultimately every digital chip possible.

Quick reference
What it is
Materials (and devices built from them) whose conductivity can be controlled — a diode conducts one way only, a transistor uses a small signal to control a much larger one.
Mental model
A diode is a one-way valve. A transistor is a valve controlled by a second, smaller input — current for a BJT, voltage for a MOSFET.
Key numbers
Silicon diode forward drop ≈ 0.7 V; MOSFET is voltage-controlled (near-zero static gate current), BJT is current-controlled.
Reach for it when
Anything that needs to switch or amplify — driving a motor/LED/relay from a microcontroller pin almost always means a transistor.

The trick that makes semiconductors useful isn't the raw material — pure silicon is a mediocre insulator — it's doping: adding trace impurities that create either a surplus of free electrons (N-type) or a surplus of "holes" where an electron is missing (P-type). Every diode and transistor below is built from junctions between N-type and P-type silicon.

Diode

A diode is a single P-N junction: current flows easily one way (forward biased) and is blocked the other way (reverse biased), up to some breakdown voltage where it fails or (for a Zener diode, deliberately) conducts in reverse at a controlled voltage.

An LED (light-emitting diode) is a diode engineered to emit light when forward biased — exactly the LED wired up in Fundamentals. A regular diode is also what makes the flyback protection on an inductive load work: wired backward across the load, it blocks current in normal operation and only conducts to absorb the collapsing field's reverse spike.

Transistor — a controlled valve

A transistor is a three-terminal device where a small signal on one terminal controls a much larger current between the other two — either as a switch (fully on or fully off) or an amplifier (a small varying input becomes a larger varying output). Two families dominate: BJTs (Bipolar Junction Transistors, controlled by current) and FETs (Field-Effect Transistors, controlled by voltage) — MOSFETs are by far the most common FET.

BJT: current-controlled

A BJT has three terminals — Base, Collector, and Emitter. A small current flowing into the base allows a much larger current to flow from collector to emitter (NPN) — the ratio between them is the transistor's current gain, often called β or hFE, commonly in the range of tens to a few hundred. Because it's current-controlled, driving a BJT means supplying real base current, which a microcontroller pin can usually do directly for small transistors, but the base current still has to be sized correctly with a resistor.

MOSFET: voltage-controlled

A MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) has three terminals — Gate, Drain, and Source. The gate is electrically insulated from the rest of the device by a thin oxide layer (literally what "metal-oxide" describes) — so a MOSFET is controlled by voltage, not current, and draws essentially zero static current into its gate. Enough gate-to-source voltage (above a threshold, VGS(th)) opens a conductive channel between drain and source; below it, the channel is closed.

N-channel
Turns on with a positive gate voltage — by far the more common type for switching a load to ground (low-side switching).
RDS(on)
The channel's resistance once fully on — directly sets how much power the MOSFET itself dissipates as heat at a given current (P = I² × RDS(on)).
Logic-level MOSFET
Specifically rated to fully turn on from 3.3 V or 5 V logic — a "standard" MOSFET may need 10 V+ on the gate to reach its rated RDS(on), which a microcontroller pin can't supply on its own.

Wiring a MOSFET as a switch

This is the single most common way an embedded engineer touches a MOSFET: using it to let a low-power microcontroller pin switch a higher-current or higher-voltage load it could never drive directly.

With the GPIO pin high, gate voltage exceeds VGS(th), the channel opens, and current flows from the +12 V rail through the motor, through the MOSFET, to ground. Drive the pin low and the channel closes — the motor stops.

Real-world applications

  • Motor and relay drivers — the low-side MOSFET switch above is close to the default circuit for letting a microcontroller control anything drawing more current than a GPIO pin can supply.
  • PWM LED dimming — a MOSFET switching fast enough (paired with PWM) controls LED brightness by varying on-time rather than current directly.
  • Switching power supplies (buck/boost converters) — the MOSFET is the actual switch rapidly connecting and disconnecting the inductor described in Passive Components.
  • Rectifier bridges — four diodes arranged in a bridge convert AC to pulsating DC, the first stage of nearly every mains power supply.
  • Reverse-polarity and ESD protection — a diode placed across a power input can protect a whole board from a battery connected backward.

Gotchas

Field notes
  • A MOSFET gate left floating (e.g. a GPIO pin in high-impedance mode during MCU reset/boot) can drift to an unpredictable voltage and partially turn the MOSFET on — a pull-down resistor from gate to source is standard practice specifically to guarantee "off" by default.
  • Driving a "standard" (non-logic-level) MOSFET directly from 3.3 V logic can leave it only partially on — high RDS(on) at partial drive means real, sometimes surprising, heat.
  • A MOSFET's gate oxide layer is thin enough to be genuinely ESD-sensitive — anti-static handling isn't just a formality for these parts.
  • Skipping the flyback diode on an inductive load (see Passive Components) is one of the most common ways to destroy a switching MOSFET or BJT.
