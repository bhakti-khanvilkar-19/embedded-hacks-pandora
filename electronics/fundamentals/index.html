Fundamentals — voltage, current, resistance, and how circuits work

Three quantities and one equation explain most of what happens in a circuit. Everything else in this notebook — every sensor, every protocol, every peripheral — is these three things arranged in different shapes.

Electric charge and current direction

Everything here starts with electric charge — a property of electrons (negative) and protons (positive). Current is charge in motion. There's one famous historical wrinkle worth knowing by name: conventional current is defined as flowing from + to − through a circuit, but the electrons actually doing the moving flow the opposite way, − to +. This convention was fixed before anyone knew what an electron was, and it stuck — every schematic, every formula, every datasheet you'll ever read uses conventional current (+ to −), so that's what this page uses too.

Voltage

Voltage is the difference in electrical potential between two points — it's what pushes charge through a circuit, and it's always measured between two points, never at just one.

Resistance and Ohm's Law

Resistance is a material's opposition to current flow. The relationship between all three quantities is Ohm's Law, the single most-used equation in electronics: 

V = I × R
I = V / R
R = V / I

Power

Power is the rate energy is used or dissipated — usually as heat in a resistive component. It matters for battery life, heat dissipation, and component ratings (a resistor rated for less power than it actually dissipates will overheat and fail). 

P = V × I
P = I² × R
P = V² / R

A complete circuit

Current only flows in a closed loop — a complete path from a power source's positive terminal, through components, back to its negative terminal. Break the loop anywhere and current stops everywhere, not just past the break.

AC vs. DC

Worth knowing

Everything above describes DC (Direct Current) — voltage and current that stay a constant polarity, exactly what a battery or a microcontroller's power rail provides. AC (Alternating Current) — like household mains power — periodically reverses direction, described by a frequency (Hz) in addition to a voltage. Almost everything else in this notebook, from sensors to microcontrollers, operates on DC; AC matters mainly at the power-supply boundary where mains power gets converted down to a stable DC rail.

Real-world applications

  • Current-limiting resistors — the LED circuit above is the single most common first circuit anyone builds, and the resistor's job — protecting the LED from too much current — shows up constantly wherever a component needs protecting.
  • Voltage dividers — two resistors in series (see series circuits above) are the standard way to scale down a voltage for a microcontroller's ADC input.
  • Battery life estimation — P = V × I directly gives the current draw a battery has to supply, and from there, roughly how long a battery of known capacity will last.
  • PCB trace sizing — P = I² × R is exactly why high-current traces need to be wider — too narrow, and I²R heating can damage the board.

Gotchas

Field notes
  • A voltmeter is placed in parallel (across a component); an ammeter is placed in series (breaking the circuit to insert it) — wiring an ammeter in parallel is a classic beginner mistake that can short the circuit.
  • "Ground" isn't automatically 0 V in an absolute sense — it's just the reference point a circuit's other voltages are measured against. Two circuits with different ground references can have a dangerous voltage between their "grounds."
  • Resistance isn't always constant — some components (a lightbulb filament, a diode) have resistance that changes with current or temperature, so Ohm's Law only applies to genuinely "ohmic" components across the range being used.
